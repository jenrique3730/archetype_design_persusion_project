---

## 1. What It Is
* **Definition:** A revolutionary German design school founded by Walter Gropius in 1919 that merged fine art, craft, and industrial technology.
* **Essential Characteristics:**
  * "Form follows function"—unnecessary decorative ornament is eliminated.
  * Rational geometric primitives (circle, triangle, square).
  * Integration of craft and mass-production manufacturing.
  * Primary colors (red, blue, yellow) alongside neutral black and white.
  * Asymmetrical balance and dynamic typography.

---

## 2. When to Use It
* **Target Audience:** Modern industrial, tech, architectural, or consumer brands focusing on functional utility, accessibility, and timeless durability.
* **Appropriate Situations:** Industrial product identity, architectural exhibitions, and functionalist hardware design.

---

## 3. How to Apply It
* **Typography:** Bold, geometric sans-serif typefaces (e.g., Futura, Bayer Universal type).
* **Color Palette:** Pure primary triad (Red `#E53E3E`, Blue `#3182CE`, Yellow `#ECC94B`) grounded by black and off-white.
* **Layout:** Dynamic diagonals, heavy rules, and structured geometric blocks.
* **Tone:** Progressive, industrial, utilitarian, and democratic.

---

## 4. Historical Context & Connection to Modernist Ideas
* Founded in Weimar, Germany, Bauhaus sought to rebuild European visual culture after World War I. It served as the foundation of modern design theory, rejecting historicist ornamentation in favor of designs optimized for industrial manufacturing.

---

## 5. Real-World Examples
* **Wassily Chair (Marcel Breuer):** Tubular steel construction inspired by bicycle frames, stripping the traditional armchair down to bare structural lines.
* **Bauhaus Building Dessau Signage (Herbert Bayer):** Universal lowercase sans-serif lettering integrated directly into functional architectural facades.
